📽️ Revision slideshow: Circles

1. Parts of a circle

A circle is the set of all points that are the same distance from a fixed point, the centre. Learn these words, because exam questions use them without explaining them:

PartMeaning
Centre (O)The fixed middle point
Radius (r)A line from the centre to the circle. All radii of a circle are equal.
Diameter (d)A chord through the centre. d = 2r and r = d ÷ 2. It is the longest chord.
ChordA straight line joining any two points on the circle
Circumference (C)The whole distance around the circle (its perimeter)
ArcPart of the circumference
SectorThe region between two radii and an arc (a "pizza slice"). A semicircle is half a circle, a quadrant is a quarter.
SegmentThe region between a chord and an arc
TangentA straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point. It meets the radius there at 90°.

2. Circumference and π (pi)

For every circle, if you divide the circumference by the diameter you get the same number, about 3.14. This number is called π (pi). It goes on forever (3.14159…), so we use π ≈ 3.14 or π ≈ 22/7. Use 22/7 when the radius or diameter is a multiple of 7.

C = π × d
C = 2 × π × r
Example 1: Find the circumference of a circle with radius 7 cm (use π = 22/7).
C = 2 × 22/7 × 7 = 2 × 22 = 44 cm
Example 2 (working backwards): A circular track has circumference 314 m. Find its radius (π = 3.14).
C = 2πr → 314 = 2 × 3.14 × r → 314 = 6.28 r → r = 314 ÷ 6.28 = 50 m
Example 3 (wheels): A bicycle wheel has diameter 70 cm. How far does the bike travel in 100 turns? (π = 22/7)
One turn = πd = 22/7 × 70 = 220 cm. 100 turns = 22,000 cm = 220 m.
Reverse question: How many turns to travel 1.1 km? 1.1 km = 110,000 cm, so 110,000 ÷ 220 = 500 turns.

3. Area of a circle

Area = π × r² (r² means r × r)
Common traps: (1) If you're given the diameter, halve it first. (2) Square the radius only: π × r², not (π × r)². (3) Area is in square units (cm², m²); circumference is in plain units (cm, m).
Example 1: Area of a circle with diameter 20 cm (π = 3.14). r = 10 cm. A = 3.14 × 10 × 10 = 314 cm²
Example 2 (semicircle): Area of a semicircle with radius 14 cm. A = ½ × 22/7 × 14 × 14 = ½ × 616 = 308 cm²
Example 3 (ring / path): A circular pond of radius 5 m has a path 2 m wide around it. Find the area of the path.
Outer radius = 5 + 2 = 7 m. Outer area = 3.14 × 49 = 153.86 m². Pond = 3.14 × 25 = 78.5 m².
Path = 153.86 − 78.5 = 75.36 m²
Example 4 (from area to radius): A circle has area 154 cm². Find the radius (π = 22/7).
22/7 × r² = 154 → r² = 154 × 7 ÷ 22 = 49 → r = 7 cm

What happens when the radius doubles?

The circumference doubles (C = 2πr has r once). The area becomes four times as big (A = πr² has r twice: 2 × 2 = 4). If the radius triples, the area becomes 9 times bigger. That's why a 40 cm pizza has 4 times the pizza of a 20 cm one.

4. Shapes made with circles

  • Perimeter of a semicircle = half the circumference + the diameter = πr + 2r. Students often forget the straight edge.
  • Perimeter of a quadrant = ¼ × 2πr + 2r.
  • For compound shapes, add the areas of the parts (or subtract a cut-out). For perimeter, trace only the outside edge.

✅ Quick check: Circles

1. Radius 21 cm. Find the circumference and area (π = 22/7).

C = 2 × 22/7 × 21 = 132 cm. A = 22/7 × 21 × 21 = 1,386 cm².

2. Find the perimeter of a semicircle with diameter 14 cm.

Curved part = ½ × π × 14 = ½ × 22/7 × 14 = 22 cm. Add the diameter: 22 + 14 = 36 cm.

3. A wheel turns 250 times to travel 550 m. Find its radius (π = 22/7).

One turn = 550 ÷ 250 = 2.2 m = 220 cm. 2πr = 220 → r = 220 × 7 ÷ 44 = 35 cm.

4. A square sheet 14 cm × 14 cm has the largest possible circle cut from it. What area is left?

The circle's diameter is 14, so r = 7. Square = 196 cm². Circle = 22/7 × 49 = 154 cm². Left = 42 cm².

2. Solving equations: the balance method

An equation is like a balanced scale. You may add, subtract, multiply or divide both sides by the same number, and it stays balanced. Use the inverse (opposite) operation to undo what has been done to x:

One-step equations

x + 9 = 15 → x = 15 − 9 = 6
y − 4 = 11 → y = 11 + 4 = 15
6n = 42 → n = 42 ÷ 6 = 7
m ÷ 5 = 8 → m = 8 × 5 = 40

Two-step equations

Undo the + or − first, then the × or ÷.

4x − 7 = 25
Add 7 to both sides: 4x = 32
Divide both sides by 4: x = 8
Check: 4 × 8 − 7 = 32 − 7 = 25 ✓
x/3 + 2 = 9
Subtract 2: x/3 = 7
Multiply by 3: x = 21

Equations with brackets

3(x + 4) = 27
Method 1: Divide both sides by 3: x + 4 = 9 → x = 5
Method 2: Expand: 3x + 12 = 27 → 3x = 15 → x = 5

Letters on both sides

5x + 3 = 2x + 18
Subtract 2x from both sides (move the smaller x term): 3x + 3 = 18
Subtract 3: 3x = 15 → x = 5
Check: 5(5) + 3 = 28 and 2(5) + 18 = 28 ✓

Negative and fraction answers are allowed

2x + 11 = 5 → 2x = −6 → x = −3
4x = 10 → x = 2.5 (or 5/2)
Always check: put your answer back into the original equation. If both sides are equal, you're right. This takes 10 seconds and catches most mistakes.

2. Formulas

Example 1 (triangle): Base 12 cm, height 7 cm. A = ½ × 12 × 7 = 42 cm².
Example 2 (parallelogram): Base 15 m, perpendicular height 8 m, slanting side 10 m. A = 15 × 8 = 120 m². The 10 m side is not used for area, only for perimeter: P = 2(15 + 10) = 50 m.
Example 3 (trapezium): Parallel sides 9 cm and 15 cm, height 6 cm. A = ½ × (9 + 15) × 6 = ½ × 24 × 6 = 72 cm².
Example 4 (working backwards): A rectangle has area 96 cm² and length 12 cm. Width = 96 ÷ 12 = 8 cm. Perimeter = 2(12 + 8) = 40 cm.
Example 5 (square from perimeter): A square has perimeter 36 m. Side = 36 ÷ 4 = 9 m. Area = 81 m².

3. Compound shapes

  1. Split the shape into rectangles/triangles with dashed lines (or treat it as a big shape minus a cut-out).
  2. Find any missing lengths by adding or subtracting the sides you know.
  3. Find each area and add (or subtract).
  4. For perimeter, go round the outside edge and add every side. Don't include the dashed lines.

4. Paths and borders

Path inside: A 20 m × 15 m lawn has a 2 m path running inside its edge. Inner lawn = (20 − 4) × (15 − 4) = 16 × 11 = 176 m². Path = 300 − 176 = 124 m².
Cross paths: A 30 m × 20 m park has two 2 m wide paths crossing in the middle, parallel to the sides. Path area = 30 × 2 + 20 × 2 − 2 × 2 = 60 + 40 − 4 = 96 m². Subtract the overlap square once, because it was counted twice.

5. Cost problems

Tiling: A floor 6 m × 4 m is covered with square tiles of side 50 cm. Each tile costs 12 AED.
Floor = 24 m². Tile = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m². Tiles = 24 ÷ 0.25 = 96. Cost = 96 × 12 = 1,152 AED.
Fencing: A rectangular field 45 m × 30 m is fenced at 18 AED per metre, with a 3 m gate that needs no fence. Fence = 2(45 + 30) − 3 = 147 m. Cost = 147 × 18 = 2,646 AED.
Painting a wall with a window: Wall 5 m × 3 m, window 1.5 m × 1.2 m. Paint area = 15 − 1.8 = 13.2 m². At 25 AED per m², cost = 330 AED.

📽️ Revision slideshow: Statistics

1. Data and averages

Data is information collected, usually as numbers. Raw data is unorganised; we organise it in tables and graphs. An average is one number that represents a whole set of data. There are three types, plus the range, which measures spread.

MeasureHow to find itGood for
MeanAdd all values ÷ number of valuesUses every value, but is affected by outliers
MedianPut the values in order; take the middle one. If there are two middle values, take their mean.Not affected much by outliers
ModeThe value that occurs most often. There can be more than one, or none.Non-number data (favourite colour), most popular size
RangeLargest − smallestShows how spread out or consistent the data is
Median position: with n values in order, the median is the (n + 1) ÷ 2th value. For 9 values, the 5th. For 10 values, halfway between the 5th and 6th.

2. Worked examples

Example 1: Marks: 12, 15, 9, 15, 18, 11, 15, 13.
In order: 9, 11, 12, 13, 15, 15, 15, 18 (8 values)
Mean = 108 ÷ 8 = 13.5 • Median = (13 + 15) ÷ 2 = 14 • Mode = 15 • Range = 18 − 9 = 9
Example 2 (missing value from the mean): The mean of 5 numbers is 12. Four of them are 10, 15, 8 and 14. Find the fifth.
Total = mean × count = 12 × 5 = 60. Known total = 47. Fifth = 60 − 47 = 13.
Example 3 (new mean): The mean height of 4 students is 150 cm. A 160 cm student joins. New mean = (600 + 160) ÷ 5 = 152 cm.
Example 4 (mean from a frequency table): Goals scored in 20 matches:
Goals (x)01234
Matches (f)36542
x × f0610128
Total goals = 36. Mean = 36 ÷ 20 = 1.8 goals. Mode = 1 (highest frequency). Median: the 10th and 11th values are both 2, so the median is 2.

3. Outliers and choosing the right average

An outlier is a value much bigger or smaller than the rest. It pulls the mean towards it.

Salaries in a small shop (AED/month): 3,000, 3,200, 3,500, 3,800, 25,000 (the owner).
Mean = 38,500 ÷ 5 = 7,700. Median = 3,500. The mean suggests a "typical" worker earns 7,700, which is misleading because four out of five earn under 4,000. The median represents the typical worker better.
  • Use the mean when values are fairly even with no outliers (e.g. average temperature).
  • Use the median when there are outliers (house prices, salaries).
  • Use the mode for the "most popular" (shoe size to stock, favourite sport).
  • Compare range for consistency: a batter scoring 40, 42, 38 (range 4) is more consistent than one scoring 0, 100, 20 (range 100), even if their means are similar.

4. Tables and bar graphs

  • Use tally marks (𝍸 = 5) to count, then write the frequency.
  • A bar graph has bars of equal width with equal gaps, labelled axes, a scale that starts at 0, and a title.
  • A double bar graph compares two sets (e.g. boys and girls) side by side with a key.
  • Probability (chance): the probability of an event = number of favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. The chance of rolling a 4 on a die = 1/6.

✅ Quick check: Statistics

1. Find the median of 7, 3, 9, 1, 12, 5.

Order: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 12. Middle two are 5 and 7, so the median is 6.

2. The mean of 6 numbers is 9. When one number is removed, the mean becomes 8. Which number was removed?

Total before = 54. Total after = 5 × 8 = 40. Removed = 14.

3. Data: 2, 4, 4, 5, 50. Which average best represents it, and why?

The median (4) or mode (4). The 50 is an outlier that pushes the mean up to 13, which doesn't represent most of the values.

4. What is the probability of picking a red ball from a bag with 3 red, 5 blue and 2 green balls?

3 ÷ 10 = 3/10 (0.3).

📽️ Revision slideshow: Angles & Constructions

1. Types of angles

An angle measures an amount of turn, in degrees (°). A full turn = 360°. Angles are measured with a protractor: put the centre on the vertex, line up 0° with one arm, and read the scale that starts at 0.

  • Complementary angles add up to 90°. The complement of 35° is 55°.
  • Supplementary angles add up to 180°. The supplement of 35° is 145°.
  • Angles are named with three letters, with the vertex in the middle: ∠ABC has its vertex at B.

2. Angle facts you must know

  • Angles on a straight line add to 180°.
  • Angles around a point add to 360°.
  • Vertically opposite angles (formed by two crossing lines) are equal.
  • Angles in a triangle add to 180°. Angles in a quadrilateral add to 360°.
  • The exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles.
  • Isosceles triangle: two equal sides, so the two base angles are equal. Equilateral: all angles 60°. Right-angled: one angle 90°, so the other two add to 90°.
Example 1: Two angles of a triangle are 48° and 57°. Third = 180 − 48 − 57 = 75°. Exterior angle next to the 57° = 180 − 57 = 123° (check: 48 + 75 = 123 ✓).
Example 2 (isosceles): The top angle of an isosceles triangle is 40°. Each base angle = (180 − 40) ÷ 2 = 70°.
Example 3 (algebra): Angles on a straight line are 2x + 10 and 3x − 5. 5x + 5 = 180 → 5x = 175 → x = 35. The angles are 80° and 100°.
Exam tip: Always give a reason for each step, e.g. "angles on a straight line add to 180°" or "vertically opposite angles are equal". Reasons often carry marks.

3. Parallel lines

A line that crosses two parallel lines is a transversal. It creates 8 angles, but only two different sizes: every acute angle is equal, every obtuse angle is equal, and acute + obtuse = 180°.

  • Corresponding ("F") angles are equal.
  • Alternate ("Z") angles are equal.
  • Co-interior ("C" or "U") angles add to 180°.

4. Constructions (ruler and compasses)

A construction is an accurate drawing made with a ruler (straight edge) and a pair of compasses. Never rub out the construction arcs, because they show your method and earn marks.

  • Perpendicular bisector of a line AB: a line that cuts AB in half (at the midpoint M) at 90°. Every point on it is the same distance from A and B.
  • Angle bisector: a line that cuts an angle into two equal halves.
  • 60°: as shown. 30° = bisect a 60° angle. 90° = construct a perpendicular. 45° = bisect a 90° angle. 120° = two 60° arcs in a row.
  • Triangle from 3 sides (SSS): draw the longest side, then swing arcs of the other two lengths from each end. A triangle is only possible if the two shorter sides add to more than the longest side (e.g. 3, 4, 8 is impossible).
  • SAS (two sides and the angle between): draw one side, measure the angle with a protractor, mark the second side along it, join up. ASA (two angles and the side between): draw the side, measure both angles at its ends, extend the arms until they meet.

✅ Quick check: Angles

1. Find the complement and supplement of 64°.

Complement = 26°; supplement = 116°.

2. Three angles of a quadrilateral are 85°, 95° and 110°. Find the fourth.

How to solve hard Grade 7 problems

  1. Read twice. Underline what the question asks and circle the numbers.
  2. List the information given, and spot anything irrelevant (added to distract you).
  3. Draw a diagram for anything about shapes, paths, angles or journeys.
  4. Choose the formula or relationship, and write it down before substituting.
  5. Work one step per line with units.
  6. Check units match (cm with cm, m² with m²). Convert first if needed.
  7. Ask: does the answer make sense? (A person can't be 300 years old; a wall can't need 0.4 tiles.)
  8. Write a final answer sentence.

A difficult question is usually several easy ideas joined together. The skill is deciding which idea comes first.

Safety: Never taste or touch lab chemicals to test them. Strong acids and strong bases are both corrosive: they burn skin. Use indicators instead. When diluting an acid, always add acid to water, slowly, never water to acid.

📽️ Revision slideshow: Light

1. How light behaves

  • Light is a form of energy that lets us see. Objects that give out their own light (the Sun, a bulb, a candle) are luminous. Most objects (the Moon, a book) are non-luminous: we see them because they reflect light into our eyes.
  • Light travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation) and is very fast: about 3 lakh km per second.
  • A ray is the path of light shown as a line with an arrow. A beam is a bundle of rays.

2. Reflection and its laws

Reflection is the bouncing back of light when it hits a surface. Smooth, shiny surfaces like mirrors give regular reflection, so you see a clear image. Rough surfaces like paper scatter light in all directions, giving irregular (diffused) reflection, so there is no image.

  • Normal: an imaginary line at 90° to the mirror at the point where the ray hits.
  • Angle of incidence (i): between the incident ray and the normal. Angle of reflection (r): between the reflected ray and the normal.
  • Law 1: ∠i = ∠r. Law 2: The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same plane.
Common trap: If a ray makes 30° with the mirror, the angle of incidence is 90 − 30 = 60°, not 30°. A ray hitting the mirror along the normal (i = 0°) bounces straight back.

3. Images in a plane mirror

  • Lateral inversion means the left and right sides are swapped. Your right hand appears as the image's left hand.
  • If you stand 2 m in front of a mirror, your image is 2 m behind it, so it is 4 m from you. If you walk 1 m towards the mirror, the distance between you and your image shrinks by 2 m.
  • Uses: dressing mirror, periscope (two mirrors at 45° let submarine crews see above water), kaleidoscope (mirrors at angles make repeating patterns).

White light is a mixture of seven colours: VIBGYOR (violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red). A prism splits them (dispersion). Raindrops do the same and make a rainbow, which is why you see rainbows when the Sun is behind you after rain. A spinning Newton's disc painted with the seven colours looks white, which proves it.

✅ Quick check: Light

1. A ray strikes a mirror at 25° to its surface. What is the angle of reflection?

Angle of incidence = 90 − 25 = 65°, so the angle of reflection = 65°.

2. Why is AMBULANCE written backwards on the front of ambulances?

Because of lateral inversion. Drivers ahead see it the right way round in their mirrors.

3. Why are convex mirrors used as car side mirrors?

They always give an upright image and show a much wider field of view.

4. Can you see a book in a completely dark room? Explain.

No. The book is non-luminous. We see it only when light reflects off it into our eyes.

📽️ Revision slideshow: Shadows

1. How shadows form

A shadow is a dark area formed when an opaque object blocks light. Because light travels in straight lines, it can't bend around the object. You need three things: a light source, an opaque object, and a screen (a wall or the ground) to see it on.

  • A shadow is always on the opposite side of the object from the light.
  • A shadow is always black/dark, whatever the colour of the object. It shows the object's outline (shape) only, not its colour or details.
  • Transparent objects make almost no shadow. Translucent objects make a faint shadow.

2. What changes the size of a shadow?

3. Shadows from the Sun

  • In the morning the Sun is low in the east, so shadows are long and point west.
  • At noon the Sun is highest, so shadows are shortest.
  • In the evening the Sun is low in the west, so shadows are long and point east.
  • A sundial uses the moving shadow of a stick to tell the time.
  • Eclipses are giant shadows. In a solar eclipse the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and its shadow falls on Earth. In a lunar eclipse the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.

4. Pinhole camera

Light from the top of the object travels in a straight line through the tiny hole to the bottom of the screen, and light from the bottom goes to the top. So the image is inverted. This is more proof that light travels in straight lines. A bigger hole makes the image brighter but blurrier.

✅ Quick check: Shadows

1. A red ball and a blue ball of the same size are held in front of a torch. Describe their shadows.

Both shadows are dark and circular, the same size if at the same distance. A shadow never shows colour.

2. At 4 pm your shadow points east. Where is the Sun?

In the west. The shadow is on the opposite side of the light.

3. In a shadow investigation, what is the independent variable if you change the lamp's distance and measure the shadow's height?

The lamp distance (it's what you change). The shadow height is the dependent variable.

📽️ Revision slideshow: Electricity

1. Electric circuits

Electric current is the flow of electric charge. It can only flow around a complete, closed path called a circuit. A cell supplies the energy. Current flows from the positive (+) terminal, through the wires and components, back to the negative (−) terminal.

  • Closed circuit: complete path, so current flows and the bulb lights. Open circuit: a gap anywhere, so no current flows.
  • A switch opens or closes the circuit. It doesn't make electricity; it just completes or breaks the path.
  • Conductors let current pass: metals like copper, aluminium and iron, plus graphite and impure water. Insulators don't: plastic, rubber, wood, glass, dry air. Wires are copper (a good conductor) covered in plastic (an insulator) for safety.

Scientists draw circuits with symbols in a circuit diagram: straight lines for wires, drawn as a neat rectangle. In a cell symbol, the longer thin line is + and the shorter thick line is −.

Why doesn't the bulb light? (Troubleshooting)

  1. Is the switch closed?
  2. Is the cell flat, or connected the wrong way in a battery (+ must join −)?
  3. Is the bulb fused (broken filament)?
  4. Is any wire loose or broken?

Test one thing at a time by swapping that part for one you know works.

2. Heating effect of electric current

  • When current flows through a wire, the wire gets hot. How hot depends on the material, length and thickness of the wire, and on the current.
  • A bulb's thin tungsten filament gets so hot (over 2,000 °C) that it glows. Much of the energy is wasted as heat, which is why LEDs and CFLs are more efficient.
  • Fuse: a thin wire that melts when too much current flows (a short circuit or overload), breaking the circuit and preventing fires. MCBs (miniature circuit breakers) do the same job and can be switched back on.
  • Always use ISI-marked appliances and never touch switches with wet hands.

3. Magnetic effect of electric current

A wire carrying current behaves like a magnet. It deflects a compass needle placed near it (discovered by Hans Christian Oersted). Coil insulated wire around an iron nail and pass current through it, and you get an electromagnet. It is a magnet only while current flows. That's its big advantage: it can be switched on and off.

Electric bell: pressing the switch makes the electromagnet attract an iron strip with a hammer, which hits the gong. This breaks the circuit, so the magnet switches off and the strip springs back, which completes the circuit again. The cycle repeats very fast, so the bell rings continuously.

✅ Quick check: Electricity

1. In a series circuit with two bulbs, one bulb fuses. What happens and why?

Both go out. There is only one path, so the gap stops current everywhere.

2. Why are house lights wired in parallel?

Each light has its own path, so you can switch them separately, and if one fails the others still work.

3. Why is a fuse wire made thin with a low melting point?

So it heats up and melts quickly when the current is too high, breaking the circuit before wires overheat and cause a fire.

4. Give two ways to make an electromagnet stronger.

More turns of wire; more cells (bigger current); use a soft-iron core.

📽️ Revision slideshow: Digestion

1. Nutrition in humans

Humans take in food in five steps: ingestion (taking food in), digestion (breaking it into small, soluble substances), absorption (into the blood), assimilation (body cells using it to build and get energy) and egestion (removing undigested waste).

Mechanical digestion physically breaks food into smaller pieces (chewing, churning), which increases its surface area. Chemical digestion uses enzymes in digestive juices to break big molecules into small ones: starch → glucose, proteins → amino acids, fats → fatty acids and glycerol.

The alimentary canal is a long tube, about 9 m: mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → rectum → anus. The liver, gall bladder and pancreas are glands that add juices but food does not pass through them.

2. Organ by organ

3. Teeth and health

  • We get two sets: milk teeth (20, which fall out from about age 6–8) and permanent teeth (32).
  • Tooth decay: bacteria feed on sugar left in the mouth and make acids that dissolve the hard enamel. Brush twice a day with (basic) toothpaste, floss, and limit sweets and fizzy drinks.
  • Diarrhoea (watery stools, often from infection) can cause dangerous loss of water and salts (dehydration). First aid is ORS (oral rehydration solution: boiled cooled water + sugar + salt).
  • Fibre (roughage) from vegetables, fruit and whole grains isn't digested, but it helps food move and prevents constipation.

4. Digestion in other animals (quick view)

  • Grass-eaters (ruminants) such as cows, goats and buffaloes swallow grass quickly into the rumen (first part of the stomach), where it is partly digested (cud). Later they bring it back to the mouth and chew the cud (rumination). Bacteria in the rumen digest cellulose, which humans can't.
  • Amoeba (single-celled) traps food with pseudopodia ("false feet") in a food vacuole and digests it there.

✅ Quick check: Digestion

1. Where is digestion completed and where is most food absorbed?

Both in the small intestine (absorption through the villi).

2. How are villi adapted for absorption?

Millions of finger-like folds give a huge surface area; thin walls; a rich supply of blood capillaries.

3. Why doesn't the stomach digest itself?

Its lining releases mucus, which protects it from the acid and digestive juices.

4. What is the function of bile? Where is it made and stored?

It breaks fats into small droplets for easier digestion. It is made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder.

Memory trick: "I change the Independent, I measure the Dependent, I Control the rest." A test is fair only if just ONE variable changes.

Reliability: repeat each test (e.g. 3 times) and calculate a mean. Repeats help you spot anomalous results (readings that don't fit the pattern). Accuracy means using the right instruments carefully: read a thermometer at eye level, start and stop the stopwatch at the right moments.

2. Results, graphs and conclusions

  • Record results in a table: independent variable in the first column, with units in the headings (Temperature (°C) | Time (s)).
  • Draw a line graph when both variables are numbers. Put the independent variable on the x-axis (horizontal). Use even scales, plot neatly, and draw a smooth line or curve of best fit.
  • Use a bar chart when the independent variable is a category (types of material, brands).

Writing a strong conclusion

  1. State the pattern: "As the temperature increased, the dissolving time decreased."
  2. Use numbers as evidence: "At 20 °C it took 80 s, but at 60 °C only 32 s."
  3. Explain using science: "At higher temperatures water particles move faster and collide with the sugar more, so it dissolves faster."
  4. Comment on reliability: "The reading at 40 °C (75 s) was anomalous, so it should be repeated."
  5. Suggest an improvement: "Use a water bath to keep the temperature steady; repeat each test three times."
Spot the unfair test: Three groups test dissolving at different temperatures, but one uses crushed sugar and another uses a sugar cube. The test is unfair because particle size also changed, so you can't tell whether temperature or size caused the difference.

3. Analysing diagrams

Follow part → function → interaction → result. Identify the system (a circuit, the digestive system), name each labelled part, say what it does, then explain how the parts work together and what happens if one fails.

1. Weather and climate: what's the difference?

The atmosphere is the blanket of air around the Earth. Its condition changes all the time. Sometimes it is hot and sunny, sometimes cloudy and windy, sometimes stormy.

Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time: temperature, air pressure, wind, humidity, clouds and rain. It can change from hour to hour and day to day. "It is 38 °C and humid in Dubai this afternoon" is a statement about weather.

Climate is the average weather of a large area, worked out over a long period, usually 30 years or more. "Dubai has a hot desert climate with very little rain" is a statement about climate.

Memory trick: Climate is what you expect. Weather is what you get.

The study of weather is called meteorology, and the scientists are meteorologists. In India, the India Meteorological Department (IMD), set up in 1875 with its headquarters in New Delhi, records the weather and makes forecasts.

3. Temperature

The Sun is the main source of heat for the Earth. The energy that reaches the Earth from the Sun is called insolation (incoming solar radiation).

The air is not heated directly by the Sun's rays passing through it. The rays first heat the ground, and the ground then heats the air touching it, a bit like a hot pan heating the air above it. That's why the air near the ground is warmer than the air higher up.

Factors that affect temperature

  1. Latitude (distance from the equator): Near the equator the Sun's rays fall almost vertically, so their heat is concentrated on a small area and it is hot. Towards the poles the rays fall at a slant and spread over a larger area, so it is colder.
  2. Altitude (height above sea level): Temperature falls by about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m of height. This is called the normal lapse rate. Hill stations like Shimla, Mussoorie, Darjeeling and Ooty are cool even in summer.
  3. Distance from the sea: Land heats and cools quickly, but water heats and cools slowly. Places near the sea have a moderate (equable) climate: Mumbai is never extremely hot or cold. Places far inland have an extreme (continental) climate: Delhi is very hot in summer and cold in winter.
  4. Cloud cover: Clouds block sunlight by day, so cloudy days are cooler. At night they act like a blanket and stop heat escaping, so cloudy nights are warmer. Deserts have clear skies, which is why they are very hot by day and cold at night.
  5. Winds and ocean currents: Warm winds and currents raise temperature, and cold ones lower it.

Working with temperature

Daily (diurnal) range = Maximum temperature − Minimum temperature
Mean daily temperature = (Maximum + Minimum) ÷ 2
°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32
Worked example: In Jaisalmer one day, the maximum is 44 °C and the minimum is 26 °C.
Daily range = 44 − 26 = 18 °C. Mean daily temperature = (44 + 26) ÷ 2 = 35 °C.
In Mumbai the same day the max might be 33 °C and min 27 °C, a range of only 6 °C, because the sea keeps the temperature moderate.

A Six's maximum–minimum thermometer records the highest and lowest temperatures of the day. Thermometers are kept in a Stevenson screen so that direct sunlight does not give a false (too high) reading.

4. Air pressure

Air has weight. The weight of the column of air pressing down on a unit area of the Earth's surface is called air pressure (atmospheric pressure). It is measured with a barometer in millibars (mb). Average pressure at sea level is about 1,013 mb.

  • Pressure falls with height. Higher up there is less air above you, so the air is "thin". Mountaineers carry oxygen cylinders, and water boils below 100 °C on high mountains, so food takes longer to cook.
  • Temperature and pressure are linked. When air is heated, it expands, becomes light and rises, creating low pressure (L). When air cools, it becomes dense and heavy and sinks, creating high pressure (H).
  • Low pressure usually brings clouds, wind and rain (rising air cools and forms clouds). High pressure usually brings clear, calm, dry weather.

On weather maps, lines joining places with the same pressure are called isobars.

5. Wind

Wind is moving air. It always blows from an area of high pressure to an area of low pressure. The bigger the difference in pressure, the stronger the wind.

Naming winds: A wind is named after the direction it comes FROM. A "west wind" (westerly) blows from the west towards the east. A wind vane's arrow points towards the direction the wind is coming from.

Types of winds

  1. Permanent (planetary) winds blow in the same direction all year: the trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies.
  2. Seasonal winds change direction with the season. The most important example is the monsoon winds of India (see Climate of India).
  3. Local winds blow over a small area for a short time:
    • Loo: a hot, dry, dusty wind that blows in the afternoons over north and north-west India in May and June. It can cause heatstroke.
    • Land and sea breezes blow daily along coasts.

Land breeze and sea breeze

By day, land heats faster than the sea. Air over the land rises (low pressure), and cooler air from the sea (high pressure) blows in to replace it. This is the sea breeze, which keeps coastal cities like Mumbai and Chennai pleasant in the afternoon.

At night, land cools faster than the sea. Now the sea is warmer (low pressure) and the cooler land air (high pressure) blows out to sea. This is the land breeze. Fishermen traditionally sail out early in the morning with the land breeze and return in the afternoon with the sea breeze.

Cyclones are violent storms that form around a centre of very low pressure over warm seas. Winds spiral inwards at great speed, anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere, bringing heavy rain and storm surges. The Bay of Bengal coast (Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal) is often hit by cyclones.

6. Humidity

Water from seas, lakes, rivers and plants evaporates and enters the air as invisible water vapour. The amount of water vapour in the air is called humidity.

  • Warm air can hold more water vapour than cold air.
  • Relative humidity compares how much vapour the air has with the maximum it could hold at that temperature, shown as a percentage. 100% means the air is saturated (completely full).
  • When the humidity is high, sweat does not evaporate easily from our skin, so we feel sticky and uncomfortable. That's why a humid 35 °C day in Mumbai or Dubai feels worse than a dry 35 °C day in Delhi.
  • Humidity is measured with a hygrometer. The wet-and-dry bulb type has two thermometers: one dry, one wrapped in wet cloth. The bigger the difference between them, the drier the air.

When moist air cools, it can no longer hold all its water vapour. The vapour turns into tiny water droplets. This is condensation. The temperature at which this happens is the dew point. Condensation forms dew (on grass), fog and mist (near the ground), frost (when below 0 °C) and clouds (high in the sky).

7. Clouds

8. Precipitation and types of rainfall

When cloud droplets join together and become too heavy to stay in the air, they fall as precipitation: rain, snow, sleet (rain and snow mixed) or hail (balls of ice). Rainfall is measured with a rain gauge.

For rain to form, moist air must rise and cool. There are three ways air can be made to rise, giving three types of rainfall:

  1. Convectional rainfall: The Sun heats the ground strongly. The air above becomes hot and rises in currents (convection currents). As it rises it cools, condenses and forms tall cumulonimbus clouds, giving heavy rain with thunder, usually in the afternoon. Common in equatorial regions every day, and in India in the hot season (pre-monsoon storms).
  2. Orographic (relief) rainfall: Moist winds blowing from the sea meet a mountain range and are forced to rise. They cool and give heavy rain on the side facing the wind, the windward side. By the time the air crosses the mountains and sinks down the other side, it has lost its moisture and is warming up, so the far side, the leeward side, gets little rain. This dry area is called a rain-shadow area.
    Best Indian example: The Western Ghats. Mumbai (windward) gets about 240 cm of rain a year. Pune (leeward, only about 150 km away) gets only about 70 cm. Most of India's monsoon rain is orographic.
  3. Cyclonic (frontal) rainfall: When a warm, moist air mass meets a cold air mass, the lighter warm air is pushed up over the heavier cold air. It cools and gives rain. In India, the western disturbances that bring winter rain to Punjab and Haryana, and cyclones from the Bay of Bengal, give this type of rain.

9. Measuring and forecasting the weather

A weather station is a place where weather instruments are kept and readings are taken at fixed times every day. Thermometers and the hygrometer are kept inside a Stevenson screen:

  • It is a white wooden box. White reflects sunlight so the box doesn't heat up.
  • It has louvres (slatted sides) so air can flow through freely.
  • It stands on legs about 1.2 m above the ground so heat from the ground doesn't affect the reading.
  • Its door faces north in the northern hemisphere so the Sun never shines directly on the instruments when it is opened.

The rain gauge, wind vane and anemometer are kept in the open. Today, weather is also tracked by satellites (such as India's INSAT series), weather radars and weather balloons. Computers use all this data to make weather forecasts.

Why weather forecasts matter

  • Farmers decide when to sow, water and harvest.
  • Fishermen are warned not to go to sea when a cyclone is coming.
  • Pilots and ships plan safe routes.
  • Disaster management: early cyclone warnings let authorities evacuate people. When Cyclone Phailin hit Odisha in 2013, nearly a million people were moved to safety in advance, saving many lives.
  • Everyday planning: sports matches, school trips, travel.

✅ Quick check: Understanding Weather

1. Why is the air near the ground warmer than the air higher up?

Because the Sun's rays heat the ground first, and the ground then heats the air in contact with it. Air farther from the ground gets less heat.

2. A place at 2,000 m has a temperature of 15 °C. Estimate the temperature at sea level nearby.

Temperature falls about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m, so 2,000 m means about 13 °C less. Sea level ≈ 15 + 13 = 28 °C.

3. Why do deserts have such a large daily range of temperature?

Clear skies and dry sand: by day nothing blocks the Sun, so the sand heats quickly; at night there are no clouds to trap the heat, so it escapes quickly and the temperature drops sharply.

4. Explain why Pune gets much less rain than Mumbai.

Mumbai is on the windward side of the Western Ghats, where moist monsoon winds rise, cool and drop heavy rain. Pune is on the leeward side, in the rain shadow; the air reaching it has lost most of its moisture and is sinking and warming, so there is little rain.

5. Name the instrument and unit for: wind speed, humidity, air pressure.

Wind speed: anemometer (km/h). Humidity: hygrometer (%). Air pressure: barometer (millibars).

📽️ Revision slideshow: Climate of India (14 slides)

1. India's climate: tropical monsoon

India has a tropical monsoon type of climate. The word monsoon comes from the Arabic word mausim, meaning season. Monsoon winds are winds that reverse their direction with the seasons:

  • In summer they blow from the sea to the land (south-west monsoon), bringing heavy rain.
  • In winter they blow from the land to the sea (north-east monsoon), and are mostly dry.

India is a very large country, so its climate varies a lot from place to place. In summer, western Rajasthan can reach 48–50 °C while Pahalgam in Kashmir is about 20 °C. In winter, Drass in Ladakh can drop to −40 °C while Thiruvananthapuram is about 25 °C. Mawsynram in Meghalaya gets about 1,180 cm of rain a year, while Jaisalmer gets only about 20 cm. Even so, the monsoon gives the whole country a common rhythm of seasons.

2. Factors that control India's climate

  1. Latitude: The Tropic of Cancer (23½° N) passes through the middle of India. The southern part lies in the tropical zone (hot most of the year). The northern part lies in the sub-tropical/temperate zone (hot summers, cold winters).
  2. The Himalayas: The high mountains in the north work like a giant wall.
    • In winter they block the freezing winds from Central Asia, so north India is not as cold as places like Mongolia at a similar latitude.
    • In summer they trap the monsoon winds, forcing them to drop their rain inside India.
  3. Altitude: Places at a height are cooler. Shimla, Darjeeling, Ooty and Munnar are hill stations.
  4. Distance from the sea: Coastal places like Mumbai, Chennai and Kochi have an equable (moderate) climate. Interior places like Delhi, Kanpur and Amritsar have an extreme (continental) climate with a big difference between summer and winter.
  5. Relief (mountains): Mountains decide where rain falls. The windward slopes of the Western Ghats get heavy rain, while the leeward Deccan Plateau is a rain-shadow area.
  6. Pressure and winds: Changes in air pressure over the land and sea cause the monsoon winds to reverse. Western disturbances (low-pressure systems from the Mediterranean region) bring winter rain to north-west India, carried by the westerly jet stream.
Equable vs extreme: Chennai's average temperature goes from about 25 °C in January to about 33 °C in May, a range of about 8 °C. Delhi goes from about 14 °C in January to about 34 °C in June, a range of about 20 °C. The sea keeps Chennai's climate moderate.

3. The four seasons of India

The India Meteorological Department divides the year into four seasons:

❄️ (a) The cold weather season (winter): December to February

  • The Sun is overhead in the southern hemisphere, so north India receives slanting rays and becomes cold.
  • Temperature falls from south to north. January averages about 10–15 °C in the northern plains but about 24–25 °C in Chennai. Frost occurs in the north, and the Himalayas get snow. Dense fog often covers the northern plains, delaying trains and flights.
  • Skies are mostly clear, days are pleasant and sunny, nights are cold, and humidity is low.
  • Winds are the north-east trade winds, which blow from land to sea, so most of India stays dry.
  • Exception 1, Tamil Nadu coast: These winds cross the Bay of Bengal and pick up moisture, so they give winter rain to the Coromandel coast (Tamil Nadu).
  • Exception 2, north-west India: Western disturbances bring light winter rain to Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and western Uttar Pradesh, and snow to the hills. This rain is called mahawat. Though small, it is very useful for rabi crops like wheat.

☀️ (b) The hot weather season (summer): March to May

  • The Sun moves north towards the Tropic of Cancer, so temperatures rise steadily. By May, north-west India reaches 45 °C or more. India's highest recorded temperature, 51 °C, was at Phalodi in Rajasthan (2016).
  • South India is less hot because of the sea's moderating effect.
  • Intense heat creates a low-pressure area over north-west India. This is very important because it later pulls in the monsoon winds.
  • Loo: strong, hot, dry winds blow in the afternoons over the northern plains, and dust storms are common in Rajasthan.
  • Pre-monsoon showers (convectional thunderstorms):
    • Mango showers: in Kerala and Karnataka, they help mangoes ripen early.
    • Blossom showers: in Kerala and nearby areas, they make coffee plants flower.
    • Kalbaisakhi (Nor'westers): violent evening thunderstorms in West Bengal and Assam. They are useful for tea, jute and rice.

🌧️ (c) The south-west monsoon season (rainy season): June to September

How it starts: In early summer a strong low-pressure area forms over north-west India. The south-east trade winds of the southern hemisphere are pulled towards it. When they cross the equator they turn right (because of the Earth's rotation) and become south-west winds. They have travelled thousands of kilometres over the warm Indian Ocean, so they are full of moisture. These are the south-west monsoon winds.

Onset: The monsoon reaches the Kerala coast around 1 June. It arrives suddenly with thunder, lightning and very heavy rain. This is called the "burst of the monsoon". It reaches Mumbai and Kolkata around 10–11 June, Delhi around the end of June, and covers the whole country by early July.

As the winds approach India, they split into two branches:

  1. Arabian Sea branch:
    • Strikes the Western Ghats. The windward western slopes and the west coast get very heavy rain (over 250 cm in places).
    • The Deccan Plateau, on the leeward side, gets much less: a rain-shadow area.
    • Part of this branch moves north along the Gujarat coast into Rajasthan, but gives very little rain there. The Aravalli hills run parallel to the wind direction, so nothing forces the air to rise. That's a major reason for the Thar Desert.
  2. Bay of Bengal branch:
    • Moves north-east towards Myanmar and north-east India. The funnel-shaped Garo, Khasi and Jaintia hills of Meghalaya force the moist air up sharply, so Mawsynram (about 1,180 cm a year) and Cherrapunji (Sohra) are among the wettest places on Earth.
    • The Himalayas block this branch and turn it westward along the Ganga plains.
    • Rainfall decreases from east to west along the plains as the winds lose moisture: Kolkata about 160 cm, Patna about 110 cm, Prayagraj about 90 cm, Delhi about 75 cm.

The two branches meet over the north-western plains around Delhi.

  • Breaks in the monsoon: The rain does not fall every day. There can be dry spells of one or two weeks in between, which can harm crops.
  • Uneven and uncertain: Some years bring floods (in Assam and Bihar, for example), others bring drought. The monsoon may also arrive late or withdraw early.
  • India gets about 75% or more of its yearly rain in this season. Kharif crops such as rice, cotton, jute, sugarcane and maize depend on it.
  • Tamil Nadu stays fairly dry in this season. It lies in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats for the Arabian Sea branch, and the Bay branch blows parallel to its coast.

🍂 (d) The retreating (post-) monsoon season: October and November

  • The Sun moves south and the low pressure over north India weakens, so the monsoon winds weaken and withdraw, starting from north-west India in September.
  • Skies clear, but the ground is still wet, so it is hot and humid for a while. This is called "October heat".
  • The low pressure moves to the Bay of Bengal, where tropical cyclones often form and strike the east coast (Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu), causing great damage.
  • The retreating monsoon and the north-east monsoon winds pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal and give heavy rain to the Coromandel coast. Chennai gets most of its rain in October–December, unlike Mumbai, which gets most of its rain in July.
Reading a climate graph: Bars show rainfall and the line shows temperature. Look for (1) the wettest months, (2) the hottest month, (3) the temperature range. Mumbai's July peak shows it gets SW monsoon rain. Chennai's November peak shows it gets NE / retreating monsoon rain. Chennai's temperature line is flatter than Delhi's would be, which shows an equable climate.

5. The monsoon as a unifying force

Although India's climate varies a lot, the monsoon ties the whole country together:

  • Farmers across India plan sowing and harvesting around its arrival. About half of India's farmland depends on rain alone.
  • Harvest festivals follow the seasons: Pongal, Onam, Bihu, Baisakhi, Makar Sankranti.
  • Rivers, reservoirs and groundwater are refilled, which supports drinking water and hydroelectricity.
  • A good monsoon helps the whole economy. A weak one causes drought, food shortages and higher prices.

✅ Quick check: Climate of India

1. Why does Rajasthan get so little rain from the Arabian Sea branch?

The Aravalli hills lie parallel to the direction of the south-west monsoon winds, so the winds are not forced to rise and cool; they pass over without giving much rain. The intense heat also makes the air hold on to its moisture.

2. Why does Chennai receive most of its rain in October–December?

During the retreating / north-east monsoon, winds blowing from the north-east cross the Bay of Bengal, pick up moisture and drop it on the Coromandel coast. In June–September, Tamil Nadu lies in a rain shadow and the Bay branch blows parallel to its coast.

3. Give two ways the Himalayas affect India's climate.

(1) They block cold winds from Central Asia in winter. (2) They trap the monsoon winds and force them to drop their rain over India, and turn the Bay branch westward along the Ganga plains.

4. What are mango showers and Kalbaisakhi?

Pre-monsoon thunderstorms in the hot season. Mango showers fall in Kerala and Karnataka and help mangoes ripen. Kalbaisakhi (Nor'westers) are violent evening storms in West Bengal and Assam, which help tea, jute and rice.

5. What is "October heat"?

After the monsoon withdraws, skies are clear and the Sun is strong, but the land is still moist. The high temperature and high humidity make the weather oppressive in October.

1. Map skills: the basics

A map is a drawing of the Earth's surface, or part of it, on a flat surface, drawn to scale. A globe shows the whole Earth correctly but is hard to carry and can't show small details. Maps can show any area in detail.

Every map has three main components:

  1. Distance (scale): the ratio between distance on the map and real distance on the ground. It can be shown three ways:
    • Statement: "1 cm represents 50 km"
    • Representative fraction (RF): 1 : 5,000,000
    • Linear (bar) scale: a line divided into marked distances
    A large-scale map shows a small area in great detail (a town plan). A small-scale map shows a big area with less detail (a map of India).
  2. Direction: Most maps have an arrow pointing North. The four cardinal directions are North, South, East and West. The four intermediate directions are North-East, South-East, South-West and North-West.
  3. Symbols: Conventional signs and colours stand for real features: blue for water, a dotted line for a boundary, a triangle for a peak. The key or legend explains them.

Types of maps

  • Physical (relief) maps show natural features: mountains, plateaus, plains, rivers, deserts, seas.
  • Political maps show countries, states, boundaries, capitals and cities.
  • Thematic maps show one theme: rainfall, crops, population, roads, minerals.
Worked example (scale): On a map with scale 1 cm = 50 km, Delhi and Jaipur are 5.4 cm apart. Real distance = 5.4 × 50 = 270 km.

3. The physical map of India: six physical divisions

⛰️ (1) The Himalayan Mountains (north)

The Himalayas are young fold mountains stretching about 2,400 km from Jammu & Kashmir to Arunachal Pradesh like an arc. They have three parallel ranges:

  • Himadri (Greater Himalayas): the highest, average height about 6,000 m, always covered in snow. Kanchenjunga (8,586 m) in Sikkim is the highest peak in the Indian Himalayas. Glaciers here (like Gangotri) are the source of rivers like the Ganga.
  • Himachal (Lesser Himalayas): 3,700–4,500 m. Famous valleys (Kashmir, Kangra, Kullu) and hill stations (Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital, Darjeeling).
  • Shiwaliks (Outer Himalayas): the lowest, southernmost range, with flat valleys called duns (for example, Dehradun).

North of them lie the Trans-Himalayas (Karakoram, Ladakh and Zaskar ranges). K2 (Godwin Austen, 8,611 m) in the Karakoram is shown on Indian maps as the highest peak of India. In the east, the Himalayas turn south as the Purvanchal (Patkai, Naga, Manipur and Mizo hills).

Importance: They block cold winds, bring monsoon rain, feed perennial rivers, have forests and wildlife, attract tourists and pilgrims, and form a natural frontier.

🌾 (2) The Northern Plains

  • Formed by alluvium (fine soil) deposited by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra and their tributaries over millions of years.
  • Very flat and fertile, with plenty of water: India's "food bowl" for wheat, rice and sugarcane. Very densely populated.
  • Divided into the Punjab plains (west, by the Indus tributaries), the Ganga plains (middle) and the Brahmaputra plains (Assam, east).
  • The Ganga–Brahmaputra delta (Sundarbans) is the largest delta in the world, home of the Royal Bengal tiger.

🏜️ (3) The Thar (Great Indian) Desert

Lies to the west of the Aravalli hills, mainly in western Rajasthan. Sandy, with dunes, very little rain (under 25 cm in most parts), and extreme temperatures. The Luni is its only significant river. (See the Thar Desert tab for full notes.)

🪨 (4) The Peninsular Plateau

  • The oldest landmass of India, made of hard ancient rocks. It was part of the old supercontinent Gondwana. Triangular in shape.
  • The Narmada river divides it into two parts:
    • Central Highlands (north of the Narmada): the Malwa Plateau, Bundelkhand and the Chota Nagpur Plateau. Chota Nagpur is very rich in minerals like coal and iron ore. Bordered by the Aravalli range (north-west, one of the oldest fold mountains in the world) and the Vindhya range.
    • Deccan Plateau (south of the Narmada): bordered by the Satpura range (north), the Western Ghats (west) and the Eastern Ghats (east). It is higher in the west and slopes gently eastward, so most rivers (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri) flow east into the Bay of Bengal. The black soil of the Deccan is excellent for cotton.
  • Western Ghats (Sahyadri): continuous, higher, cause heavy orographic rain. Anamudi (2,695 m) in Kerala is the highest peak of south India.
  • Eastern Ghats: broken into separate hills by the east-flowing rivers, and lower. The two Ghats meet in the Nilgiri Hills (Doddabetta, 2,637 m; Ooty).
  • Narmada and Tapi are the exceptions. They flow west into the Arabian Sea through rift valleys (long cracks) between the Vindhya and Satpura ranges.
  • The Meghalaya Plateau in the north-east is an extension of the Peninsular Plateau.

📽️ Revision slideshow: The Thar Desert (12 slides)

1. Location and extent

  • The Thar Desert, also called the Great Indian Desert, lies in north-west India, west of the Aravalli Range.
  • Most of it is in western Rajasthan. Small parts extend into Gujarat (up to the Rann of Kutch), Haryana and Punjab, and it continues across the border into Pakistan (Sindh and Cholistan).
  • Total area: about 2 lakh km², the largest part in India. It is one of the most densely populated deserts in the world.
  • Important towns: Jaisalmer, Bikaner, Jodhpur, Barmer.

2. Climate: why is the Thar a desert?

  • Very low rainfall: between about 10 and 50 cm a year. Most areas get under 25 cm, and Jaisalmer gets about 20 cm. Rain falls in a few heavy showers in July–August and is very unreliable: some years almost none. Rainfall increases from west to east, towards the Aravallis.
  • Extreme temperatures: Summer days can exceed 45–50 °C (Phalodi recorded 51 °C). Winter nights can drop to near 0 °C. The daily range is very large: the dry sand and clear skies heat up fast by day and lose heat fast at night.
  • Hot, dry winds (loo) and dust storms (andhi) are common in May–June.
  • Low humidity and clear skies for most of the year.

Reasons for the dry climate

  1. The Aravallis lie parallel to the Arabian Sea branch of the south-west monsoon. The winds are not forced to rise, so they pass over the region without giving much rain.
  2. The Bay of Bengal branch has already dropped most of its moisture over eastern and northern India by the time it reaches Rajasthan.
  3. The intense heat raises the air's capacity to hold moisture, so even moist air does not easily condense.
  4. There are no high mountains in the west to block and lift the winds.

3. Landforms and drainage

  • Mostly sandy plains covered with sand dunes, mounds of sand built up by the wind. Some dunes move with the wind (shifting dunes) and can bury roads and fields.
    • Barchans are crescent-shaped dunes with their "horns" pointing downwind.
    • Longitudinal dunes are long ridges lying along the direction of the wind.
  • There are also rocky areas, low hills and salt lakes (shallow lakes that dry out leaving salt), such as Sambhar Lake, the largest inland salt lake in India, on the eastern edge, and Didwana and Pachpadra.
  • The Luni is the only important river. It rises near Ajmer in the Aravallis and flows south-west into the Rann of Kutch. It is seasonal (flows mainly after rain), and its water becomes salty downstream. "Luni" comes from the Sanskrit for "salt river".
  • The Rann of Kutch in Gujarat is a huge salt marsh that floods in the monsoon and becomes a white salt desert in the dry season.

4. Natural vegetation and wildlife

Desert plants are xerophytes, plants adapted to save water:

  • Long roots reach deep groundwater. Small, thick or waxy leaves, or thorns, reduce water loss. Fleshy stems store water.
  • Common plants: khejri (Rajasthan's state tree; its pods, called sangri, are eaten), babool (acacia), ber, rohida, ker (kair) bushes, cactus, and sewan grass, a nutritious fodder.

Animals:

  • Camel: the "ship of the desert". Its hump stores fat for energy. It can go days without water and then drink a lot quickly. Wide padded feet stop it sinking into sand. Long eyelashes and closable nostrils keep out sand.
  • Great Indian Bustard: Rajasthan's state bird. A large ground bird that is critically endangered, protected in the Desert National Park near Jaisalmer and Barmer.
  • Chinkara (Indian gazelle, Rajasthan's state animal), blackbuck, desert fox, desert cat, spiny-tailed lizard, snakes, and peacocks.

5. People and their way of life

The Thar is home to many communities: Rajputs, Bishnois, Jats, Meghwals, the Rabari (Raika) camel and sheep herders, and folk musicians such as the Manganiyars and Langas. Many people live in small villages called dhanis.

The Bishnois, India's first environmentalists: Their faith forbids cutting green trees and killing animals. In 1730 at Khejarli, near Jodhpur, Amrita Devi and 363 Bishnois gave their lives hugging khejri trees to stop them being cut. This inspired the later Chipko movement. Blackbuck and chinkara still roam freely near Bishnoi villages.

Adapting to the desert

  • Houses: thick mud walls and small windows keep the inside cool by day and warm at night. Roofs are flat or conical and thatched, and walls are often whitewashed or plastered with mud and cow dung.
  • Clothes: loose, light cotton clothes. Men wear turbans (pagdi) and women cover their heads with an odhni, which protect against sun and sand. Bright colours stand out against the plain desert.
  • Food: millets like bajra (rotis), dal-baati-churma, ker-sangri; lots of milk and buttermilk.
  • Saving water: water is precious, so people harvest rainwater in tankas/kunds (covered underground tanks), johads and nadis (village ponds), and baoris (step-wells).

1. The Constitution

A constitution provides a framework for governing a country. It sets up institutions, powers, rights and principles. India's Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950 (Republic Day) and is the supreme law of the country.

Real life: If a government authority wants to take an action, its power is not unlimited. Laws and constitutional principles decide what authorities may and may not do.

2. The Preamble

The Preamble is the introduction to the Constitution. It declares India a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic, and sets out the ideals of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity.

Scenario: If two students are prevented from joining an activity for an unfair reason, the idea of equality helps you judge the decision. If a policy tries to correct a disadvantage, it is more complicated than "everyone must always be treated identically."

3. Rights and duties

Fundamental Rights protect important freedoms: equality, freedom (of speech, movement…), freedom of religion, rights against exploitation, cultural and educational rights, and the right to constitutional remedies (going to court). Fundamental Duties are responsibilities, like respecting the national flag, protecting the environment and promoting harmony.

Real life: You can express an opinion, but responsible citizenship includes respecting other people and following laws. Rights are not permission to harm others.

4. Government institutions

The legislature (Parliament, State Assemblies) makes laws. The executive (Prime Minister, ministers, officials) carries them out. The judiciary (courts, with the Supreme Court at the top) interprets laws and settles disputes.

Case: A law is passed, a ministry runs a programme to implement it, and a court later decides a dispute about whether an action follows the law. These are three different roles that are connected.

5. Community values and source analysis

Respect, tolerance, responsibility, cooperation and empathy only mean something when they show in how people behave. When given a source, identify its main claim, pick out evidence, explain what it suggests, and link it to a civic principle.

2. How colours agree with clothes

  1. The colour comes AFTER the noun (the opposite of English): قميص أحمر = a red shirt (literally "shirt red").
  2. Feminine nouns take feminine colours: تنورة حمراء (a red skirt), سترة زرقاء (a blue jacket).

  4. Plurals of things (not people) take the feminine singular colour: جوارب بيضاء (white socks), الأحذية السوداء (the black shoes).

📽️ Revision slideshow: Reflexive Verbs

1. What is a reflexive verb?

A reflexive verb (verbe pronominal) describes an action that the person does to themselves. In the dictionary it has se in front: se laver = to wash (oneself). The se changes to match the subject. This little word is the reflexive pronoun.

Compare: Je lave la voiture. = I wash the car (someone else). Je me lave. = I wash (myself).

Formula: subject + reflexive pronoun + verb (conjugated normally).

3. Negatives and questions

Negative: ne goes before the pronoun, pas goes after the verb. The pronoun stays glued to the verb.

Je ne me lève pas tôt le dimanche. (I don't get up early on Sundays.)
Il ne se couche jamais avant 22h. (He never goes to bed before 10 pm.)
Tu ne t'habilles pas ? (Aren't you getting dressed?)

Questions: raise your voice (Tu te lèves à quelle heure ?) or use Est-ce que (Est-ce que tu te douches le matin ?).

With another verb (aller, vouloir, devoir + infinitive), the pronoun matches the subject: Je vais me coucher (I'm going to go to bed). Nous devons nous dépêcher (We must hurry).

Bonus, passé composé: reflexive verbs use être, and the past participle agrees: Je me suis levé(e) à 7h. Elle s'est habillée. Nous nous sommes amusés.

1. What is the imperative?

The impératif is used to give commands, instructions, advice and invitations: classroom instructions, recipes, signs, directions. It has only three forms: tu (one person you know), nous ("let's…") and vous (a group, or one person politely). You don't say the subject.

1. How to write a strong paragraph

1. What tense tells us

The tense of a verb shows when an action happens (past, present or future) and how we see it: as a simple fact, as ongoing (continuous), or as completed and connected to another time (perfect).

📽️ Revision slideshow: Dialogue Writing

1. What good dialogue does

Dialogue is a conversation between characters. In good writing it is never just "filler". Every line should do at least one job:

  • Reveal character: how someone speaks shows who they are (polite, rude, nervous, bossy, funny).
  • Move the plot forward: give information, make a decision, create a problem.
  • Create conflict or tension: disagreement makes readers want to know what happens.
  • Show relationships: friends tease, strangers are formal, a parent and child speak differently.
Show, don't tell: Instead of "Ravi was angry", write: "Don't touch my things," Ravi snapped, slamming the drawer shut.

2. Punctuation rules (story format)

  • Use a new paragraph every time the speaker changes.
  • Don't overuse "said". Choose verbs that show how something is said: whispered, shouted, muttered, insisted, laughed, sighed, admitted, demanded, pleaded. But "said" is fine sometimes. Don't make every line dramatic.
  • Add actions (beats) between lines: She folded her arms. "Fine."
  • Keep speech natural: short sentences, contractions (I'm, don't), and interruptions shown with a dash (—) or trailing off (…).

3. Script (play) format

If the question asks for a dialogue in script form, use no speech marks. Write the speaker's name, a colon, then the words. Stage directions go in brackets and italics.

Setting: A school corridor after the science fair results are announced.

Zaid: (waving the results sheet) We came fourth. Fourth! I told you the volcano was a bad idea.
Aisha: The volcano worked. It was the explanation that was weak.
Zaid: Because you wrote it in five minutes!
Aisha: (quietly) Because you spent three days painting the volcano.
Zaid: (pauses) …Okay. That's fair. Next time I'll do the research and you do the model?
Aisha: (smiling) Deal. But I'm choosing the colour.

Why this works: each speaker has a clear attitude (Zaid is dramatic and blaming; Aisha is calm and honest), there is conflict, the problem is solved, and the last line ends with a touch of humour.

4. Exam checklist for dialogue

  1. Does it suit the situation and the characters (formal or informal)?
  2. Does it have a beginning (greeting/situation), a middle (problem/discussion) and an end (decision/closing)?
  3. Does each speaker have a distinct voice?
  4. Are punctuation and new lines correct?
  5. Did you stay within the word/line limit?
📽️ Revision slideshow: Travelogue

1. What is a travelogue?

A travelogue is a piece of writing about a journey or a place someone has visited. It mixes information (where, when, what you saw) with personal experience (what you felt, learnt and remember). It should make the reader feel they travelled with you.

Features

  • Written in the first person ("I", "we"), usually in the past tense.
  • Chronological order (the journey in sequence) with time connectives: at dawn, by noon, later that evening.
  • Vivid sensory details: sights, sounds, smells, tastes, textures.
  • Facts about the place: history, food, culture, people.
  • Figurative language: similes, metaphors, personification.
  • Reflection: what the trip meant to you or what you learnt.

2. Structure

PartWhat to write
TitleCatchy and specific: "Golden Dunes and Silver Stars: Two Days in Jaisalmer"
Introduction (hook)Start in the middle of a moment, with a sound, an image or a question. Say where you went and why.
Body paragraphsOne paragraph per place or part of the day. Describe with the senses, include a fact, and say how you felt.
ConclusionReflect: what stayed with you, what you learnt, whether you would return.

3. Model travelogue

A Morning in Old Dubai

The abra's engine coughed twice, then settled into a steady putter as we pulled away from the Bur Dubai side of the Creek. Spray flicked across my arm, cool for a moment against the already-warm air. It cost just one dirham to cross, yet the water taxi felt like a doorway into another century.

On the Deira side, the Spice Souk swallowed us whole. Sacks of turmeric glowed like little suns, and dried limes, black and wrinkled, rattled when the shopkeeper scooped them. The air was thick with cinnamon and cardamom, so rich I could almost taste it. "Smell this," the trader said, pressing a pinch of saffron into my palm. "From Iran. The best." I believed him.

The Gold Souk next door was quieter but dazzling. Window after window blazed with bangles and necklaces, as if someone had melted the sun and poured it into shapes. My younger brother pressed his nose to the glass until Mum gently pulled him away.

By eleven, the heat had become a heavy blanket, so we retreated to Al Fahidi, where narrow lanes are shaded by tall wind towers called barjeel. Before air conditioning, these towers caught passing breezes and pushed them down into the houses. Standing beneath one, I felt a faint, surprising coolness and marvelled at how clever people had been without any electricity.

As the abra carried us back, the glass towers of modern Dubai shimmered on the horizon. I realised the city I thought I knew had an older heart, one that smelled of spices and moved at the speed of a wooden boat.

Why it scores well: opens in action with sound (onomatopoeia "putter"); every paragraph uses senses; includes facts (one dirham, barjeel wind towers); similes ("glowed like little suns"); dialogue for life; time connectives (By eleven, As the abra…); a reflective ending that links back to the start.

1. P.A.W.S.: writing an analytical paragraph

Each letter is a sentence (or two) in your paragraph:

  • P – Point: a clear claim that answers the question. Use the question's words.
  • A – Analysis: explain what the evidence shows or suggests. Use "this suggests…", "this shows…".
  • W – Writer's method: name the technique and embed a short quotation (a few words, in quotation marks).
  • S – Significance: why it matters: the effect on the reader, the mood created, or the link to the poem's theme or message.

Model P.A.W.S. paragraph (The Tyger)

Question: How does Blake present the tiger as frightening?

[P] Blake presents the tiger as a terrifying creature that must have been made by a powerful and dangerous creator. [A] The speaker imagines the tiger being built in a blacksmith's workshop, which suggests it is strong, hard and made with violent force rather than gentle love. [W] He uses a series of rhetorical questions with metalwork imagery, "What the hammer? what the chain, / In what furnace was thy brain?", and the repeated hard sounds of "hammer", "chain" and "dread grasp" make the lines sound heavy, like blows on an anvil. [S] This makes the reader feel awe and fear, and because the questions are never answered, we are left as uncertain and amazed as the speaker, wondering what kind of creator could make something so beautiful yet so deadly.
Top tips: Keep quotations short and embedded. Analyse single words ("burning", "dread") for their connotations. Always end with the effect on the reader. Avoid "This makes the reader want to read on", which is too general.

The Tyger – William Blake (1794)

Tyger Tyger, burning bright,
In the forests of the night;
What immortal hand or eye,
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

In what distant deeps or skies,
Burnt the fire of thine eyes?
On what wings dare he aspire?
What the hand, dare seize the fire?

And what shoulder, & what art,
Could twist the sinews of thy heart?
And when thy heart began to beat,
What dread hand? & what dread feet?

What the hammer? what the chain,
In what furnace was thy brain?
What the anvil? what dread grasp,
Dare its deadly terrors clasp!

When the stars threw down their spears
And water'd heaven with their tears:
Did he smile his work to see?
Did he who made the Lamb make thee?

Tyger Tyger burning bright,
In the forests of the night:
What immortal hand or eye,
Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?

Background: The poem comes from Blake's Songs of Experience. It is a partner to "The Lamb" in Songs of Innocence, which shows a gentle, innocent creation. "The Tyger" shows the frightening, powerful side of creation.

Glossary: frame = make, shape; symmetry = perfectly balanced form; deeps = depths of the sea; aspire = rise up; sinews = tendons, muscles; anvil = iron block a blacksmith hammers metal on; immortal = living forever (a god).

Stanza-by-stanza meaning

StanzaMeaningKey technique
1The speaker addresses the tiger, glowing like fire in the dark forest, and asks what god could have made something so beautifully and frighteningly balanced.Apostrophe (speaking to the tiger), repetition "Tyger Tyger", fire imagery "burning bright", contrast of light and dark, and the contrast inside "fearful symmetry"
2Where did the fire in its eyes come from: the depths of the sea or the sky? What creator dared to fly up and seize that fire?Rhetorical questions; myth-like image of a daring creator (like Prometheus stealing fire)
3What strength and skill twisted the tiger's heart muscles? When its heart started beating, what terrible hands and feet did the creator have?Body imagery, the word "dread" repeated, broken questions that sound breathless
4The creator is imagined as a blacksmith with hammer, chain, furnace and anvil, daring to grip the tiger's deadly brain.Extended metaphor of a forge; short, hammering questions; harsh sounds
5When the stars gave up their weapons and cried, did the creator smile at what he made? Could the same god who made the gentle lamb make the tiger?The central question: contrast between Lamb (innocence) and Tyger (experience / danger)
6The first stanza repeated, but "Could frame" becomes "Dare frame".Circular structure; the change from could (ability) to dare (courage) makes making the tiger seem even more dangerous

Form, themes and key quotations

  • Form: 6 quatrains (4-line stanzas) in rhyming couplets (AABB). Strong, regular beat (mostly 7 syllables, stressed-unstressed, like a drumbeat or a hammer).
  • Questions: the poem contains around 13–14 questions and no answers. This shows wonder, confusion and awe.
  • Themes: the power and mystery of creation; good and evil; beauty and danger existing together; innocence vs experience; faith and doubt.
  • Tone: awed, amazed, fearful, questioning.
QuotationWhat to say about it
"burning bright"The tiger's stripes and eyes blaze like fire in the dark. It suggests energy, beauty and danger at once.
"fearful symmetry"An almost contradictory pair: symmetry is beautiful and ordered, but "fearful" makes it terrifying. The tiger is perfect and deadly.
"What the hammer? what the chain"Blacksmith metaphor: the tiger was forged with strength and heat, not gently. Short questions sound like hammer blows.
"Did he who made the Lamb make thee?"The key question: how can one creator make both gentleness and violence?
"Dare frame"Changed from "Could": making the tiger needed courage. The creator is bold, or even reckless.

The Eagle – Alfred, Lord Tennyson (1851)

He clasps the crag with crooked hands;
Close to the sun in lonely lands,
Ring'd with the azure world, he stands.

The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;
He watches from his mountain walls,
And like a thunderbolt he falls.

Glossary: clasps = grips tightly; crag = a steep, rough rock face; crooked = bent, curved (his talons); ring'd = surrounded; azure = bright sky-blue; thunderbolt = a flash of lightning with thunder.

Line-by-line

LineMeaningTechnique & effect
"He clasps the crag with crooked hands"The eagle grips the rock with its curved talons.Alliteration of hard "c/k" sounds (clasps, crag, crooked) sounds harsh and strong, like his grip. Personification: "hands" and "he" make him seem human, almost like a king.
"Close to the sun in lonely lands"He is very high up, in a remote place.Hyperbole: "close to the sun" exaggerates his height. Alliteration "lonely lands". He is powerful but isolated.
"Ring'd with the azure world, he stands."He is surrounded by blue sky.Imagery: the sky forms a ring or crown around him. "He stands" is a short, proud ending to the stanza: calm, still, in control.
"The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls"From so high, the sea's waves look like wrinkles, and they seem to move slowly.Personification and perspective: the mighty sea looks old, small and weak ("crawls") compared to the eagle.
"He watches from his mountain walls"He keeps watch from the cliffs."his mountain walls" sounds like a castle; the eagle is like a king or guard surveying his kingdom (or a hunter looking for prey).
"And like a thunderbolt he falls."He dives suddenly at great speed (probably to catch prey).Simile: speed, power, danger and suddenness. The poem ends with dramatic action after all the stillness.

Form and themes

  • Form: two tercets (3-line stanzas), rhyme scheme AAA BBB (hands/lands/stands; crawls/walls/falls). Regular iambic tetrameter (8 syllables, da-DUM ×4), which feels controlled and steady, like the eagle.
  • Structure: stanza 1 is stillness (he stands); stanza 2 moves from watching to sudden action (he falls). The final word "falls" is the climax.
  • Themes: power and majesty of nature; isolation; the hunter; perspective (how things look from above).
  • Tone: admiring, awe-struck, calm, then dramatic.
