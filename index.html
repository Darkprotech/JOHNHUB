📐 Maths Hub

Mathematics is about relationships. A formula is useful, but knowing when and why to use it is more important. The examples below deliberately connect Grade 7 mathematics to situations such as transport, construction, sports, shopping and school planning.

1. Circles: radius, diameter, circumference and area

The radius is the distance from the centre to the circumference. The diameter crosses the entire circle through its centre and is twice the radius. The circumference is the distance around the circle. The area is the surface enclosed by it.

d = 2r
C = 2πr
A = πr²

Notice that radius is squared in the area formula. If the radius doubles, the circumference doubles but the area becomes four times as large. That difference is extremely important in real problems.

Real life: A circular fountain has radius 4 m. A landscaper needs the circumference to estimate decorative edging around it. A tiler needs the area to estimate how much surface must be covered. Same fountain, different mathematical measurement.
Transport connection: A bicycle wheel with a diameter of 70 cm travels roughly one circumference every complete rotation. If the wheel rotates 10 times, the distance can be estimated from 10 × circumference.
Common trap: If a question gives diameter but the formula requires radius, divide by 2 first. Do not put the diameter directly into 2πr.

2. Equations and unknowns

An equation represents a balance. The letter is not just a random symbol: it represents a quantity that changes or is unknown.

Real life: A school event charges a fixed 10 AED registration fee plus 18 AED per student. A group pays 118 AED. Let x be the number of students. The situation becomes 10 + 18x = 118. Solving the equation tells you something about the real event.

Harder questions may require you to build the equation yourself. Look for phrases such as “per”, “fixed fee”, “more than”, “less than”, “twice”, and “total”. Then check your result by putting it back into the original situation.

3. Area and perimeter

Perimeter measures the boundary. Area measures the inside surface. A fence is usually connected to perimeter, while paint, grass, carpet or tiles are usually connected to area.

Rectangle: A = l × w
Rectangle: P = 2(l + w)
Triangle: A = ½bh
School example: A rectangular noticeboard is 2.4 m wide and 1.2 m tall. The amount of border material depends on its perimeter. The amount of paper covering its front depends on its area. Using the wrong measurement answers the wrong question.
Compound shapes: Split the shape into familiar rectangles or triangles. Label missing lengths before calculating.

4. Mean, median and range

The mean uses every value. The median depends on order. The range only depends on the smallest and largest values.

Real life: Five delivery times are 12, 14, 15, 16 and 55 minutes. The mean is affected heavily by the 55-minute delay, while the median is 15. If a company wants a number representing a typical delivery, it should think carefully about which statistic is most informative.

When calculating a median, always sort the data first. For an even number of values, the median is the mean of the two middle values.

5. Angles and constructions

Angles around a point total 360°. Angles on a straight line total 180°. Angles inside a triangle total 180°. These facts allow you to calculate unknown angles from relationships rather than guessing from a drawing.

An angle bisector divides an angle into two equal angles. A perpendicular bisector crosses a line segment at 90° and at its midpoint.

Engineering connection: A triangular support structure has to fit between fixed points. Exact angle relationships are more reliable than trying to “eyeball” the shape.

6. How to solve genuinely difficult Grade 7 problems

  1. Write what the question asks.
  2. List the information given.
  3. Ignore information that is irrelevant.
  4. Draw a diagram if the situation is spatial.
  5. Choose a formula or relationship.
  6. Work one step at a time.
  7. Check units.
  8. Ask whether the answer makes sense in real life.

A difficult question often combines several easy ideas. The challenge is deciding which idea comes first.

🔬 Science Hub

Science answers should connect a claim to evidence. When you analyse an experiment, ask what changed, what was measured, what stayed the same and what the results actually show.

1. Acids, bases, neutral substances and pH

The pH scale commonly runs from 0 to 14. Values below 7 are acidic, 7 is neutral and values above 7 are alkaline. Universal indicator gives a range of colours that can be compared with a pH chart. Litmus gives a simpler acid/alkali test.

Real life: Lemon juice is acidic. Soap solutions are commonly alkaline. Pools require controlled chemistry because water conditions can affect people, materials and equipment.

Do not confuse “acidic” with “dangerous”. A substance can be weakly acidic, while a different substance can be strongly acidic. The pH provides evidence about acidity.

2. Light and reflection

Light travels in straight lines through a uniform medium. Reflection occurs when light bounces from a surface. The normal is an imaginary line at 90° to the surface at the point where the ray hits.

angle of incidence = angle of reflection

Both angles are measured from the normal, not from the mirror surface.

Real life: A car mirror redirects light from objects behind the car toward the driver's eyes. Changing the mirror angle changes the direction of the reflected light.

3. Shadows and experimental variables

An opaque object blocks light and creates a shadow. Moving the light source can change the size and direction of the shadow.

The independent variable is deliberately changed. The dependent variable is measured. Control variables remain the same.

Investigation: A student wants to know whether lamp distance affects shadow length. The lamp distance is the independent variable, shadow length is the dependent variable, and the object height and screen position should be controlled.

4. Electricity and circuits

A circuit needs a complete conducting path. A cell supplies energy. A switch opens or closes the path. A bulb transfers electrical energy mainly into light and thermal energy.

Real life: A torch may have a working battery but no light because the switch is open or a connection is broken. Troubleshooting means checking the entire circuit.

Metals are commonly used as conductors. Plastic is often used as an insulator around electrical wires because it does not allow current to pass easily.

5. Digestion

Digestion breaks food down so useful substances can be absorbed. Mechanical digestion changes the size and shape of food. Chemical digestion changes substances into smaller molecules using processes including enzymes.

The mouth begins digestion. Food passes through the oesophagus to the stomach. The small intestine is important for continued digestion and absorption. The large intestine absorbs much of the remaining water.

Real life: Chewing a piece of bread increases its surface area. This makes it easier for digestive processes to act on the food.

6. Fair tests and data

A fair test changes one main factor while keeping other important conditions controlled. Repeating measurements helps reveal unusual readings and makes results more dependable.

Example: Three groups test dissolving time at different temperatures. If one group uses crushed sugar and another uses a whole cube, the comparison is weakened because particle size also affects dissolving.

When describing a graph, state the overall pattern, support it with numbers and identify unusual results. Do not merely say “the graph goes up”. Explain what the increase means.

7. How to analyse diagrams

First identify the system. Next identify each labelled part. Then follow arrows or pathways. Finally explain how the parts interact. A strong answer often follows part → function → interaction → result.

🌍 SST Hub

SST is about understanding how institutions and citizens interact. You will practise applying constitutional ideas to realistic situations instead of memorising isolated definitions.

1. The Constitution

A constitution provides a framework for governing a country. It can establish institutions, powers, rights and principles. India's Constitution is the supreme legal framework of the country.

Real life: If a government authority wants to take an action, its power is not unlimited. Laws and constitutional principles help establish what authorities may and may not do.

2. The Preamble

The Preamble expresses important ideals including justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. These ideas are useful tools for analysing situations.

Scenario: If two students are prevented from participating in an activity for an unfair reason, the idea of equality can be used to analyse the decision. If a policy attempts to correct a disadvantage, the question becomes more nuanced than simply saying “everyone must always be treated identically.”

3. Rights and duties

Rights protect important freedoms and opportunities. Duties describe responsibilities citizens should fulfil. A democratic society requires people to understand that freedom and responsibility exist together.

Real life: A person may express an opinion, but responsible citizenship includes respecting other people and following laws. Rights should not be treated as permission to harm others.

4. Government institutions

The legislature is associated with making laws, the executive with carrying out and administering government policy, and the judiciary with interpreting laws and resolving disputes.

Case: A law is passed, a government ministry develops a programme to implement it, and a court later considers a dispute about whether an action follows the law. These are different roles, even though they are connected.

5. Community values

Respect, tolerance, responsibility, cooperation and empathy become meaningful when they affect behaviour.

School example: A group disagrees about a project. Listening to opposing ideas demonstrates respect. Finding a workable compromise demonstrates cooperation. Completing your task demonstrates responsibility.

6. Source and data analysis

When given a passage or table, identify its main claim, select evidence, explain what the evidence suggests and connect it to the relevant civic principle. Avoid copying the source without analysis.

ع Arabic Hub

Arabic practice focuses on meaning in context, reading carefully and producing connected language. Knowing a word is useful; knowing when and how to use it is much more useful.

1. Clothes vocabulary

قميص = shirt, بنطال = trousers, فستان = dress, تنورة = skirt, سترة = jacket, معطف = coat, حذاء = shoe, جوارب = socks, قبعة = hat, وشاح = scarf, حزام = belt, تي شيرت = T-shirt.

Real life: If the weather is cold, معطف and وشاح make sense. If it is hot, a تي شيرت may be more suitable. Context gives vocabulary meaning.

2. Colours and descriptions

Useful colours include أحمر, أزرق, أخضر, أصفر, أسود, أبيض and بني. When describing clothing, combine the colour and object rather than memorising the two words separately.

Practical task: Imagine describing an outfit to a friend who cannot see it. You need enough information for the friend to picture the clothing, not just a list of vocabulary.

3. Reading comprehension

Read once for the general idea. Read again for names, objects, colours, actions and reasons. If a question asks “why”, find the cause in the passage. If it asks “what”, find the relevant object or action.

Real life: A paragraph might describe someone choosing a jacket because it is cold. A strong reader connects the weather to the clothing choice rather than merely spotting the word “jacket”.

4. Paragraph writing

A good paragraph has a clear opening, connected details and a conclusion. Use connectors so that the writing becomes a sequence of ideas rather than separate sentences.

Useful structure: Where you are → what you are wearing → colours → why you chose the clothes → what you will do next.

5. Thinking in context

When you meet an unfamiliar word, examine surrounding words. Is it an object, action, colour, person or place? Context can sometimes help you infer meaning without immediately translating every word.

🇫🇷 French Hub

French is not about memorising random sentences. Build sentences from useful structures, understand why the grammar changes and apply it to real situations.

1. Production écrite

A strong French paragraph needs a clear purpose. Start with the situation, develop details and finish naturally. Connectors such as et, mais, parce que, aussi, d'abord, ensuite, enfin help ideas flow.

Real life: If you are writing about a weekend plan, do not create five unrelated sentences. Explain what you will do first, what happens next and why you chose those activities.

2. L'impératif

The imperative is used for commands, instructions and advice. The forms commonly studied are based on tu, nous and vous. Negative commands use ne ... pas.

Real life: A teacher giving classroom instructions might use commands. A recipe uses commands such as “mix”, “add” or “wait”. A safety notice may use a negative command.

3. Reflexive verbs

Reflexive verbs describe actions done to oneself. Examples include se lever, se laver, se brosser, s'habiller, se réveiller, se coucher. The reflexive pronoun changes with the subject.

Real life: A morning routine is perfect for reflexive verbs because many actions are performed by the person on themselves.

4. Connectors and reasoning

Connectors tell the reader how ideas relate. Parce que gives a reason. Mais introduces contrast. Ensuite indicates sequence. Enfin signals a conclusion or final step.

Challenge: Compare “Je vais au parc. Il fait beau. Je joue au football.” with a connected paragraph using reasons and sequence. The second version communicates relationships rather than just facts.

5. Reading strategy

Look for familiar words, verb forms, names, time expressions and connectors. Use the surrounding sentence to infer meaning. Do not panic because of one unknown word.

📚 English Hub

English assessment is about making choices and explaining their effects. Instead of saying that a writer “uses a metaphor”, explain what the metaphor suggests and why it matters.

1. Tenses

The present simple commonly describes habits and facts. The present continuous describes something happening around now. The past simple describes completed past events. The past continuous describes an action in progress at a past time. The future can describe predictions or planned actions depending on the structure. The present perfect connects a past action to the present.

Real life: “I play football on Fridays” describes a routine. “I am playing football now” describes an action happening around the present. Choosing the wrong tense can change the meaning of a sentence.

2. Dialogue writing

Good dialogue sounds like people communicating, not two characters giving essays to each other. Use purposeful speech, reactions and punctuation. Each speaker should have a reason for speaking.

Real life: If two friends are deciding how to solve a problem, one might suggest an idea, the other might challenge it, and the first might adapt the plan. That creates movement in the conversation.

3. Travelogue writing

A travelogue combines description, experience and reflection. Include specific sensory details, but do not turn every sentence into a list of adjectives. Explain what made the place memorable.

Real life: Instead of simply saying “the market was amazing”, describe a sound, smell, sight or interaction and then explain how it affected your experience.

4. P.A.W.S.

P — Point: make your main claim. A — Analysis: explain the idea. W — Writer's Method: identify the technique or choice. S — Significance: explain why the choice matters to the reader, character or theme.

Example thinking: Do not stop at “the writer uses imagery.” Ask: what image is created, what does it suggest, and how does that affect the reader?

5. V.O.I.C.E.

V — Vocabulary: word choices. O — Organisation: structure and sequencing. I — Imagery: pictures and comparisons. C — Character/Context: who is speaking and why. E — Effect: what the reader experiences.

6. Poetry analysis

For poems such as The Tyger and The Eagle, focus on themes, imagery, sound, structure, contrast and the speaker's attitude. You do not need to memorise entire poems. You need to understand how language creates meaning.

Real-life connection: A writer choosing powerful animal imagery can make a creature seem frightening, majestic, vulnerable or mysterious depending on the vocabulary and structure used.
